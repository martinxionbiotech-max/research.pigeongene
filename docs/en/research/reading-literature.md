# How to Read Scientific Literature

Scientific literature is the core source of reliable scientific information, but reading it takes method. Knowing how to search for papers, how their structure works and how to read critically helps you get useful information efficiently and accurately.

## Plain-language view (an analogy)

Reading a research paper is like **reading a detective's report** - you cannot accept all of it from cover to cover; you read with questions in mind. What case is being investigated (the research question)? What evidence was used (the methods)? Does the conclusion stand up (the results)? Does it admit the leads it could not close (the limitations)? Learn this critical-reading routine and clickbait headlines will not lead you astray.

## Types of literature

Knowing the types of literature is the first step in reading:

- **Original research**: reports original experimental or observational results; the core source of evidence;
- **Review**: summarises research progress in a field, useful for a quick overview;
- **Textbooks and monographs**: systematic summaries of knowledge, useful for building a basic framework;
- **Conference abstracts**: preliminary results, with limited strength of evidence.

## Structure of a paper

Original research papers usually follow the IMRaD structure:

- **Introduction**: research background and aim;
- **Methods**: experimental design and technical approach;
- **Results**: data and findings;
- **Discussion**: interpretation of results, limitations, conclusion.

## Points for critical reading

Read papers with a critical mindset:

1. **Research question**: what question is the study trying to answer?
2. **Soundness of the methods**: are the experimental design and sample size adequate?
3. **Reliability of the results**: do the data support the conclusion?
4. **Limitations**: do the authors acknowledge the study's limitations?
5. **Applicability**: can the conclusion be extrapolated to racing pigeon practice?

## Common pitfalls

- **Reading only the abstract**: the abstract holds limited information and key details may be missed;
- **Over-interpretation**: reading "association" as "causation";
- **Ignoring the limitations**: overlooking constraints such as sample size and experimental conditions;
- **Over-generalising**: treating the conclusion of a single study as a general rule.

## Practical advice

1. **Start with reviews**: read a review first to build the overview, then go deeper into original research;
2. **Follow authoritative journals**: give priority to peer-reviewed authoritative sources;
3. **Cross-check**: when several papers corroborate one another, the conclusion is more reliable;
4. **Take notes**: record key conclusions, methods and limitations.
